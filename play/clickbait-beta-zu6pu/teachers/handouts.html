Subject: 9 ways to spot a phish (this email is real, and here's how you can tell)

Hi, Goose State student! (We'd use your name, but this is a handout.)

Scammers send emails and texts that look like they come from your school, your bank or your family. Most give themselves away with a few of these tells. Check for them before you click, reply or type anything.

  1. The sender's address doesn't match. Look past the name to the address itself. it-help@goosestate-edu.help is not @goosestate.edu, and a professor won't write from a Gmail account.
  2. The link goes somewhere else. Link text can say anything. Hover over it (or press and hold on a phone) to see the real address before you tap.
  3. It rushes or scares you. "Within 2 hours or your grades are deleted!" Urgency is there to stop you from thinking.
  4. A generic greeting. "Dear Student" or "Dear user" instead of your name.
  5. It asks for your password or bank details. Real IT never asks for your password by email or text. Not once, not ever.
  6. Odd spelling, grammar or formatting. "Due to a system eror your grades has been FROZEN."
  7. The login page has the wrong address. If a link takes you to a sign-in page, check the address bar before typing anything.
  8. Read addresses from the end. goosestate.edu.refund-center.co starts with the school's name, but it belongs to refund-center.co. The real owner is the last part of the name, just before the first single slash.
  9. A padlock isn't proof. 🔒 only means the connection is private. Scam sites have padlocks too.

Not sure? Don't use the link or phone number in the message. Go to the site yourself, or call the person on a number you already have. A text from "Mom's new number" is easy to check: call her old one.

Already clicked? Change your password from the real site, then tell IT (or a grown-up). It happens to everyone; reporting it fast is what matters.

Stay sharp,
Goose State IT Security

Noodle · INFO 101: Not Getting Fooled · Module 4

SIFT: four moves before you believe or share

You don't need to be an expert to check a story. You need a few minutes and these four moves. Do them in any order, and stop as soon as you know enough.

S

Stop

Notice how the post makes you feel. Angry, scared, or "I have to share this right now"? That's the moment to slow down. Ask: do I know this source? What do I want to find out?

In the game: ALL CAPS, outrage and "share before they delete this!" were the partisan sites. The calm, specific headline was usually the reliable one.

I

Investigate the source

Before you read closely, find out who's behind it. Open a new tab and look the source up: what do other people say about it? Who owns it, and what might they want? This is lateral reading: you learn more by leaving a site than by staying on it.

In the game: the ⋮ "About this source" button and Goosepedia showed who ran each site.

F

Find better coverage

Search for the claim itself, not the site. Are trusted outlets reporting the same thing? If only one site has it, or it contradicts everyone else, be careful. You don't have to save a bad source; find a better one.

In the game: every search showed several sites for the same story, plus one "Sponsored" ad on top (that's an ad, not a result).

T

Trace claims, quotes and media to the original

Follow the links back to where the claim started: the study, the meeting minutes, the full video. Does the original really say what the headline says? Watch for a study of 12 people turning into "Science PROVES…".

In the game: the original documents were real pages you could open.
